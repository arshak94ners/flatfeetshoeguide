Why plantar fasciitis and flat feet overlap so often

The plantar fascia is a thick band of tissue running along the bottom of the foot, from the heel to the base of the toes, and it plays a real role in holding the arch's shape under load. When the arch is already flattened, that band gets stretched further with every step, which is exactly the kind of repeated strain that leads to plantar fasciitis in the first place. That's why the two conditions show up together so often — it's not a coincidence, it's cause and effect.

This also explains why treating them separately doesn't always work. A shoe built purely to cushion heel pain, with no real arch support, can leave the underlying flat-foot mechanics untouched, and the fasciitis often comes back. A shoe built purely for arch support without enough cushioning under the heel can aggravate an active flare-up. The shoes above are chosen because they handle both sides of the problem at the same time.

Shoes help, but they're not the whole treatment Calf stretching, morning stretches before your first steps, and short bouts of rest from high-impact activity all matter alongside footwear. See our flat feet exercises and stretches guide for a routine that pairs well with the shoes above.

What actually matters in a shoe for both conditions

  • A firm heel counter. This locks the heel in place at the moment of impact, reducing the strain that travels into the plantar fascia.
  • Real arch support, not just a soft footbed. A supportive midsole that resists collapsing inward takes over work the fascia would otherwise have to do.
  • Enough heel cushioning to absorb impact. Especially important during an active flare-up, before other structural support even becomes relevant.
  • A slight heel-to-toe drop. A completely flat (zero-drop) shoe increases tension through the calf and plantar fascia during the healing phase; a moderate 8-12mm drop is usually more comfortable.

If pain persists for more than a few weeks despite a supportive shoe, or is severe enough to change how you walk, that's worth a visit to a podiatrist rather than trying to shoe your way out of it — see our medical disclaimer for more on where this site's advice ends and professional care should start.

Frequently asked questions

Can the right shoes alone fix plantar fasciitis?

Shoes can meaningfully reduce strain and discomfort, but plantar fasciitis usually responds best to a combination of supportive footwear, calf and foot stretching, and short-term activity modification. If pain persists for more than a few weeks, see a podiatrist.

Should I wear supportive shoes even at home?

Yes, if that's practical for you. Walking barefoot or in unsupportive slippers at home is a common reason plantar fasciitis doesn't improve despite good shoes elsewhere in the day.

Do I need a shoe or an insole, or both?

It depends on your current shoes. If they already fit well and just lack support, a rigid insole like the Superfeet GREEN can be enough. If the shoe itself is unsupportive, replacing it is usually more effective than adding an insole on top.
