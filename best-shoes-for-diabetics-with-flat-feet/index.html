Why this is different from ordinary shoe shopping

For most people, an uncomfortable shoe causes noticeable pain that prompts a change before real damage happens. Diabetic neuropathy can blunt or eliminate that warning signal, which means a seam rubbing the wrong way, a pressure point from a poor fit, or a small object inside the shoe can cause a blister or wound without ever being felt. Combined with flat feet — which already redistribute pressure unevenly across the foot — the margin for error in shoe choice gets meaningfully smaller.

What to prioritize in a shoe

  • Seamless or minimally-seamed interior. Internal seams are a common, preventable source of friction injuries.
  • Extra depth. Enough internal volume to accommodate a specialist-prescribed diabetic insole without cramming the foot.
  • Breathable materials. Reduces moisture buildup, which matters for skin integrity.
  • A wide, accommodating toe box. Reduces pressure points and lets toes sit naturally rather than crowded together.
  • A firm, stable base. Flat feet plus reduced sensation can affect balance; a stable sole reduces fall risk.

If your doctor has prescribed a certified therapeutic or diabetic shoe (sometimes called an "A5500" shoe in the US, covered in some cases by insurance), that prescription should take priority over any general recommendation on this page.

Daily habits that matter as much as the shoe

Podiatrists commonly recommend a daily visual foot check (using a mirror if needed) to catch cuts, blisters, redness, or swelling before they become serious, precisely because reduced sensation means you can't rely on feeling a problem develop. Checking the inside of shoes for debris or worn interior linings before putting them on is a similarly low-effort habit worth building.

Frequently asked questions

Is this page a substitute for a podiatrist's advice?

No. This page reflects general, non-medical footwear guidance based on public information about diabetic foot care. If you have diabetes, regular podiatrist checkups and any specific footwear guidance from your doctor should take priority over anything suggested here.

What is a 'diabetic shoe' exactly?

It typically refers to a therapeutic shoe designed with a seamless interior, extra depth, and pressure-reducing features, sometimes prescribed by a doctor and in some cases partially covered by insurance (in the US, often referred to as an A5500 shoe). Not every diabetic needs one, but they're worth asking a doctor about if you have neuropathy or a history of foot ulcers.

Why does flat feet make diabetic foot care more complicated?

Flat feet already redistribute pressure unevenly across the foot, which combined with reduced sensation from neuropathy increases the risk of an unnoticed pressure injury developing in a spot that's taking more load than it should.
