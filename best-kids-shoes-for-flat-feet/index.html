Is my child's flat foot normal?

Most toddlers look flat-footed because of a fat pad under the arch and loose ligaments, and arches typically develop through early childhood. The common pattern is a flexible flat foot: the arch disappears when standing but appears when your child stands on tiptoe or sits with their feet hanging. That's usually not a concern.

See a pediatrician or podiatrist if: your child has pain in the feet, ankles, or legs; the foot looks flat even on tiptoe (a rigid flat foot); only one foot is affected; they tire much faster than other kids; or they limp. Our flat feet in children guide explains these signs in more detail.

What to look for in a kids' shoe

  • A firm heel counter that doesn't collapse when you squeeze it.
  • Flex at the toes, not the middle. Bend the shoe: it should bend where the toes bend, not fold in the arch.
  • A secure closure. Laces or hook-and-loop straps keep the heel seated. Avoid floppy slip-ons for everyday wear.
  • Room to grow, but not too much. About a thumb's width past the longest toe. Too much room makes a child grip with their toes.
  • Replace them often. Kids outgrow shoes every few months, and hand-me-downs carry the previous child's wear pattern.

Frequently asked questions

At what age do children develop arches?

Arches usually develop gradually through early childhood, with many children showing a visible arch by around age 6. Some people keep flexible flat feet into adulthood with no problems at all.

Do kids with flat feet need orthotics?

Most children with flexible, painless flat feet don't need orthotics. If your child has pain, a rigid flat foot, or other concerns, a pediatrician or pediatric podiatrist can tell you whether orthotics or other treatment would help.

Should kids go barefoot if they have flat feet?

For healthy kids, barefoot play on safe, natural surfaces is generally encouraged for foot development. Supportive shoes matter more for long days on hard surfaces, school, and sports. Ask your pediatrician if your child has pain.
