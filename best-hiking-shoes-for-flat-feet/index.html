Best Hiking Shoes and Boots for Flat Feet

Trails are where flat feet get found out. Every rock and root asks your arch to stabilize the foot, and a soft, flexible hiker leaves it to do that alone. These picks put structure under the arch and around the heel instead.

What matters in a hiking shoe for flat feet

On pavement, a flat arch mostly costs you comfort. On a trail, it costs you stability too: every uneven step gives the foot another chance to roll inward, and that strain travels into the ankle, knee, and hip. These features do the most to prevent it:

  • A firm heel counter. Squeeze the back of the shoe. If it collapses easily, it won't control the heel when you land on a slanted rock.
  • Torsional rigidity. Hold the heel and forefoot and twist. A shoe with a shank or a stiff midsole resists this, which is what keeps the midfoot from collapsing on uneven ground.
  • A removable insole. Even the best stock footbeds are generic. A removable one means you can add an orthotic insole if you need firmer support.
  • A wide enough toe box. Flat feet often splay longer and wider under load, especially on descents. A cramped forefoot causes blisters and black toenails.
  • Mid or low cut? Mid-cut boots don't support your arch, but they do help ankles that roll. If that's you, go mid.

Fit tips before a long hike

Try hiking shoes on in the afternoon, wearing the socks you'll hike in, with your orthotic inside if you use one. Feet swell over a long day, and flat feet lengthen slightly as the arch drops under load, so a shoe that's snug in the morning can be tight by mile eight. Walk down a slope or a ramp in the store if you can: your toes shouldn't hit the front.

Break new shoes in on short local walks before any long trip, and if you're new to hiking with flat feet, the foot-strengthening exercises in our guide make a noticeable difference to how tired your feet feel at the end of the day.

Frequently asked questions

Are hiking boots better than hiking shoes for flat feet?

Not automatically. Boots add ankle support, which helps if your ankles roll, but the arch support comes from the footbed and midsole in either case. Many people with flat feet do best in a low hiker with a firm heel counter and a good insole, and only move to a mid boot for rough terrain or heavy packs.

Can I put my orthotics in hiking shoes?

Yes, as long as the shoe has a removable insole and enough volume. Take the stock insole out and put your orthotic in its place rather than stacking them. All five picks on this page have removable insoles.

Are trail runners OK for hiking with flat feet?

For light day hikes, a stable trail runner can work, but most are softer and more flexible than hiking shoes, which gives a flat arch less help on uneven ground. If you prefer trail runners, choose one with a firm heel counter and consider adding an insole.
