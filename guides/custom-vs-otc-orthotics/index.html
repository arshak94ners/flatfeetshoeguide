Custom vs. Over-the-Counter Orthotics: Which Do You Actually Need?

Custom orthotics can cost ten times more than a solid over-the-counter insole, and the honest answer is that most people with flat feet don't need to spend that much. Here's how to tell which category actually fits your situation.

On this page

  1. What's actually different
  2. When OTC is enough
  3. When custom is worth it
  4. OTC options worth trying first
  5. FAQ

What's actually different between the two

An over-the-counter (OTC) insole is manufactured in standard shapes and sizes, then sold in a few firmness or arch-height variants that you choose based on your general arch type. A custom orthotic is built from a mold or 3D scan of your specific foot, typically prescribed and fitted by a podiatrist, and priced accordingly — often $300-600 versus $30-50 for a quality OTC option.

The performance gap between the two is real but often smaller than the price gap suggests, especially for straightforward flat feet without a complicating medical condition. Custom orthotics earn their cost in specific situations, covered below, rather than being categorically "better" for everyone.

When an OTC insole is genuinely enough

  • Your flat feet are flexible (the arch reappears when you're not standing on it) rather than rigid.
  • You don't have a diagnosed complicating condition like severe plantar fasciitis, diabetes-related foot issues, or a significant leg-length discrepancy.
  • Your discomfort is mild-to-moderate rather than significantly limiting your activity.
  • You haven't tried a quality OTC option yet — it's a reasonable, low-cost first step before considering custom.

When custom orthotics are actually worth it

  • You've tried a quality OTC insole for several weeks without meaningful improvement.
  • Your flat feet are rigid rather than flexible, or asymmetric between your two feet.
  • You have a diagnosed condition like severe plantar fasciitis, diabetes with neuropathy, or a significant biomechanical issue a podiatrist has identified.
  • You're managing pain related to a specific sport or occupation where precise, individualized support meaningfully affects performance or injury risk.

In these cases, a podiatrist assessment (sometimes covered partially by insurance) is worth the cost precisely because it accounts for details a mass-produced insole can't — asymmetries, specific pressure points, and how your particular gait pattern behaves under load.

A reasonable order to try things in Start with a supportive shoe and a quality OTC insole. Give it several weeks of consistent use. If that's not enough, and especially if pain is significant or a doctor has flagged a specific condition, that's the point to look into a podiatrist consultation and custom orthotics — not the first step by default.

Frequently asked questions

Is a custom orthotic always better than an OTC insole?

Not for everyone. Custom orthotics earn their higher cost in specific situations: rigid or asymmetric flat feet, a diagnosed complicating condition, or when a quality OTC insole hasn't helped after several weeks. For straightforward, flexible flat feet, a good OTC insole is often enough.

Will insurance cover custom orthotics?

Sometimes, partially, depending on your plan and whether a podiatrist documents medical necessity. It varies enough by provider and region that checking directly with your insurer before committing is worthwhile.

How long should I try an OTC insole before considering custom?

A few weeks of consistent use is a reasonable trial period. If discomfort isn't improving by then, or is significant enough to limit your daily activity, that's a reasonable point to discuss custom orthotics with a podiatrist.
