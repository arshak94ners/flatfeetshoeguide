Flat Feet Exercises and Stretches That Actually Help

Exercise won't rebuild a bone arch that's flat in adulthood, but it can meaningfully reduce discomfort by strengthening the muscles that support the foot and calf. Here's what's realistic, and a simple routine to start with.

What exercise can (and can't) do for flat feet

It's worth being honest about this upfront: in adults, the bones and ligaments that determine arch height are largely set, and no exercise routine will structurally rebuild a collapsed arch back to a "normal" shape. What exercise can do is strengthen the muscles running through the foot, ankle, and calf that support whatever arch structure you have, which in practice often means less fatigue, less pain, and better control of excess pronation during activity. That's a genuinely useful outcome even though it's a different one than "fixing" the arch itself.

In children whose bones are still developing, flat feet often resolve naturally as arches form through normal growth and activity — see our guide on flat feet in children for that separate conversation, since the exercise expectations are different at that age.

Calf stretch

Tight calf muscles increase strain on the plantar fascia and arch with every step, so this is usually the highest-value stretch to start with. Stand facing a wall, step one foot back, keep that back heel flat on the ground, and lean forward gently until you feel a stretch through the calf. Hold 30 seconds, switch legs, repeat 2-3 times per side. Do this daily, ideally both morning and evening.

Short foot exercise

This targets the small stabilizing muscles along the arch directly. Sit with your foot flat on the floor, and without curling your toes, try to draw the ball of your foot toward your heel, shortening the foot slightly and lifting the arch a small amount. Hold 5 seconds, relax, repeat 10 times per foot. It feels subtle and slightly awkward at first — that's normal, and it gets easier to isolate the movement with practice.

Towel scrunches

Place a small towel flat on the floor, put your bare foot on one end, and use your toes to scrunch the towel toward you, then push it back out. This strengthens the intrinsic muscles along the sole of the foot. Do 2-3 sets of 10-15 scrunches per foot, a few times a week.

Consistency matters more than intensity These exercises work through gradual strength gains, not a single dramatic session. A few minutes daily for several weeks will do more than an occasional long session. Pair this with supportive footwear during the day — exercises and shoes address different parts of the same problem, not one instead of the other.

A simple starting routine

  1. Morning: Calf stretch, both sides (before your first steps, when calves are tightest).
  2. A few times a week: Short foot exercise, 10 reps per foot, plus towel scrunches, 2-3 sets per foot.
  3. Evening: Calf stretch again, both sides.

Give it 4-6 weeks of reasonably consistent practice before judging whether it's helping — strength and control changes take longer to notice than a single stretch session would suggest. If pain is significant or worsening rather than gradually easing, that's a signal to see a podiatrist or physical therapist rather than continuing to self-manage.

Frequently asked questions

Can exercises fix flat feet permanently?

In adults, exercise can't structurally rebuild a flat arch back to a different shape, since the underlying bone and ligament structure is largely set. It can meaningfully reduce discomfort and improve control by strengthening supporting muscles, which is a realistic and worthwhile goal even though it's different from a structural fix.

How long before I notice a difference?

Give a consistent routine 4-6 weeks before judging results. Strength and control improvements build gradually, unlike the immediate relief a stretch might provide in the moment.

Do these replace supportive shoes?

No, they complement them. Exercises address muscle strength and control; shoes address the mechanical support your foot needs during daily activity. Most people managing flat feet discomfort benefit from both together, not one instead of the other.
