Flat Feet in Children: What's Normal, and When to Worry

Nearly all toddlers have flat-looking feet, and most of them develop a visible arch naturally over the following years without any treatment. Here's how to tell ordinary childhood development from something actually worth a doctor's visit.

This page is general information, not a diagnosis Every child's development is different, and this page can't account for your specific child's history or exam findings. If you're worried, a pediatrician or pediatric podiatrist visit is a quick, low-stress way to get a specific answer rather than relying on general information. See our medical disclaimer.

Why almost all young children look flat-footed

Babies and toddlers are born with a fat pad in the arch area that makes the foot look flat regardless of the underlying bone and ligament structure developing underneath. On top of that, the ligaments that eventually hold an arch's shape are still loose and flexible at this age, which is entirely normal and necessary for a body that's still growing. This is why pointing out a "flat" foot on a two-year-old usually isn't meaningful information on its own — it's close to universal at that age.

What usually happens as children grow

As children grow, walk, and put weight through their feet over months and years, the arch typically develops gradually, most noticeably between about ages 3 and 10, though the exact timeline varies a lot between individual kids. By the early teenage years, most children have developed a visible arch consistent with what they'll carry into adulthood. A meaningful portion of children — often cited around 1 in 5 to 1 in 4 — will still have flexible flat feet as adults, and for most of them this causes no problems at all and needs no treatment.

Signs that actually warrant a doctor visit

Most flat feet in children need no intervention, but a few specific signs are worth getting checked rather than waiting out:

  • Rigid flat feet — if the arch doesn't reappear at all when the child stands on tiptoes, unlike the flexible flat feet that resolve naturally.
  • Pain or limping — ordinary flexible flat feet in children are usually painless; persistent foot, leg, or knee pain is worth a proper evaluation.
  • One foot noticeably different from the other — significant asymmetry between the two feet is less likely to be typical developmental variation.
  • Stiffness or reduced range of motion in the ankle or foot joints.
  • No improvement at all by around age 8-10, especially combined with any of the above.

None of these automatically mean something is seriously wrong, but they're the specific signals that shift this from "wait and see" to "worth a professional opinion," rather than general flat-footedness on its own.

A note on kids' shoes

We haven't reviewed specific children's shoes on this site, since our testing and recommendations are based on our own adult, first-hand wear — see our approach for why we're careful about that distinction. As a general principle pediatric podiatrists commonly mention: flexible, well-fitting shoes with enough room to grow and a reasonably wide toe box support natural foot development better than rigid, overly structured "corrective" footwear for a child with no diagnosed condition. If a doctor has recommended a specific orthotic or shoe type for your child, that guidance takes priority over general advice.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal for a toddler to have flat feet?

Yes, it's close to universal. Nearly all toddlers have a fat pad in the arch area and loose, still-developing ligaments that make the foot look flat regardless of how it will eventually develop. On its own, this isn't a cause for concern.

At what age should a child's arch have developed?

Development varies a lot between children, but a visible arch typically emerges gradually between about ages 3 and 10. A meaningful portion of children will still have flexible flat feet into adulthood, which for most people causes no problems.

What's the difference between flexible and rigid flat feet in children?

Flexible flat feet show a visible arch when the child stands on tiptoes or when the foot isn't bearing weight, and are usually a normal developmental variation. Rigid flat feet show no arch in either position, which is less common and more likely to warrant a doctor's evaluation.
