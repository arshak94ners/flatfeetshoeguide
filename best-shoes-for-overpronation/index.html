Overpronation and flat feet aren't the same thing

Overpronation describes what your foot does when it lands — it rolls inward more than a "neutral" amount as your weight moves from heel to toe. Flat feet describe the shape of your arch when you're standing still. The two are correlated because a collapsed arch often leads to more inward roll, but plenty of people with flat feet don't overpronate significantly, and some people with normal-looking arches do. If you've been recommended a "stability shoe for flat feet" without anyone actually watching how your foot moves, it's worth understanding this distinction before buying.

Because of that overlap, most of the shoes recommended for flat feet on this site are also solid overpronation picks and vice versa — the difference is which factor is actually driving your discomfort.

How to tell if you actually overpronate

A few practical signals, none of which require special equipment:

  • Worn-out shoe pattern. Check the outsole of an old, well-worn pair. Wear concentrated along the inner edge of the heel and forefoot is a classic overpronation signature.
  • The wet footprint test. Wet the sole of your foot and step onto a dry, dark surface. A footprint showing almost the entire sole, with little to no arch curve, points toward a flatter arch that often (not always) comes with overpronation. See our full at-home flat feet test for details.
  • Ankle position in a mirror. Stand naturally in front of a mirror and look at your ankles from behind. A noticeable inward lean of the ankle, rather than a straight vertical line, suggests overpronation during standing, which usually shows up more during walking and running too.

None of these replace a proper gait analysis at a running specialty store or a podiatrist's assessment, but they're a reasonable starting point before buying a shoe based on a guess.

Stability, not motion control, for most people Most overpronation, even when noticeable, is mild-to-moderate rather than severe. See our stability vs. neutral vs. motion control guide for how to figure out which category actually fits, rather than defaulting to the firmest option available.

Frequently asked questions

Can you overpronate without having flat feet?

Yes. Overpronation is about how the foot moves on impact, while flat feet describe arch height at rest. They're correlated but not identical, and some people with normal-looking arches still overpronate noticeably.

Do I need a podiatrist to know if I overpronate?

Not necessarily. A worn-shoe check, the wet footprint test, and a mirror check of ankle position at rest are reasonable starting signals. A running specialty store gait analysis or a podiatrist visit is more precise if you want certainty before a purchase.

Is a firmer shoe always better for overpronation?

No. Overcorrecting with a motion-control shoe when your overpronation is actually mild can feel rigid and uncomfortable without adding real benefit. Match the shoe's firmness to how significant your overpronation actually is.
